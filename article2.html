Who Is Tina Peters to Herself?

The grief, the losses, the identity she built from them — and the worldview that filled the gap. How personal trauma can lay the groundwork for radical belief.

~25 min read 7 sections
i. September 11, 1955

Tina Marie Peters was born on September 11, 1955. She grew up in the United States, in a family her supporters describe as having a strong sense of service. She became a flight attendant. She studied holistic nutrition through a correspondence course. She married a man named Tom Peters and helped, to varying degrees depending on who you ask, run his construction company in Grand Junction, Colorado. She raised a son. She lived, for most of her adult life, in the quiet middle of things — not obscure exactly, but not notable either.

There is a particular kind of ambition that doesn't announce itself until late — that waits, accumulates, and then arrives all at once. Peters was 63 years old when she first ran for public office. Most people, at 63, are winding down. She was just starting.

What drove her to run is a question that gets different answers depending on who you ask. She has said she wanted to improve service at the county's Department of Motor Vehicles — a mundane, local concern, the kind of thing that genuinely bothers people and that a county clerk can actually fix. Her supporters describe a woman who cared deeply about her community. Her critics describe a woman who wanted a platform and found one. Both may be true.

What is documented is the shape of her life before politics: unconventional, peripatetic, not easily categorized. Her degree in holistic nutrition came from Clayton College of Natural Health — a correspondence institution that was never accredited by a recognized accrediting body and has since closed. It is the kind of credential that signals something about a person's relationship to official knowledge — a preference for alternatives, a skepticism of gatekeepers, a belief that what the institutions offer isn't necessarily the whole picture. It does not make her unusual, exactly. But it is a data point.

She is not a stupid woman. Everyone who has spent time with her — supporters and critics alike — makes that clear. What she is, or became, is something harder to name: a person who found, late in life, a cause that made everything else make sense. The question this article tries to answer is how she got there.

ii. The Outsider Credential

Clayton College of Natural Health, where Peters earned her bachelor's degree in holistic nutrition, was a correspondence institution — meaning coursework was completed by mail, with no campus attendance required. The school was never accredited by a nationally recognized accrediting body. It closed in 2010.

The degree was Peters' only known academic credential when she took office as Mesa County Clerk in 2019. Colorado does not require county clerks to hold a specific educational background — the position is elected, not appointed — but the state does require clerks to complete a certification program through the Colorado County Clerks Association.

Peters never completed it.

"She does not understand the job," Matt Crane, executive director of the Colorado County Clerks Association, told the court at Peters' sentencing. "She did not attempt to master it. She did not complete the work to be a certified election official."

Former Mesa County elections coordinator Karen Delgado, who worked in the clerk's office during Peters' tenure, said the gap between Peters' credentials and the demands of the job was apparent early on.

"Elections work is technical," Delgado said. "There are federal and state statutes, there are deadlines, there are security protocols. You have to know the systems. She wasn't interested in learning any of it."

Rae, who ran the office for eight years before Peters took over, said she attempted to ease the transition by including Peters in meetings and staff interviews before she officially took office.

"None of that interested her," Rae said.

What did interest her, according to people who worked alongside her, was the political dimension of the job. Peters attended Republican events, cultivated relationships with conservative activists and positioned herself as a voice for election integrity — a term that, in the post-2020 political environment, carried significant ideological weight.

By April 2021, that positioning would bring two visitors to her office who would change the course of her tenure entirely.

iii. May 28, 2017

On May 28, 2017, Remington J. Peters jumped out of a helicopter over the Hudson River in New York City. He was a member of the U.S. Navy SEAL parachute demonstration team — the Leap Frogs — and that afternoon they were performing for a crowd of tens of thousands who had gathered for a Memorial Day air show. He was wearing a wingsuit. He was carrying colored smoke. He was 923 jumps into a career that had taken him to the most elite corners of the American military.

His primary parachute lines became entangled. He fell.

The military ruled his death was in the line of duty. He was not, technically, in combat. He was performing stunts over a river in front of a crowd, on a holiday weekend, for reasons that had everything to do with pageantry and nothing to do with war. A woman in the crowd told the New York Times it was "so unnecessary, just to entertain people, for someone to have died." She was not wrong, exactly. She was also not the one who had lost a son.

Tina Peters began calling herself a Gold Star mother. The Gold Star designation formally applies to families of service members killed in combat — in action against an enemy, on foreign soil as part of a peacekeeping force, or under other congressionally defined criteria. Remington's death, ruled as it was, did not meet those criteria. Peters does not appear to have corrected this impression when it arose in public. Her supporters call her a Gold Star mother without qualification. She has let them.

What she has done with her grief is a question that divides people who know her. Her critics say she weaponized it — that Remington became a rhetorical device, a way of generating sympathy and donations that had nothing to do with his memory and everything to do with her legal defense. Her supporters say she is a mother who lost her only son and has never fully recovered, and that to question how she mourns is indecent.

Both of these things can be true at once.

Dee Valdez Pepper met Tina Peters at a Lincoln Day dinner in Elbert County in 2022 — a small, rural Republican county between Colorado Springs and Denver. Peters was campaigning for Secretary of State. She was standing in the back of the room when Dee noticed she had lipstick on her teeth. "I just said, oh, you got a little bit of lipstick on your teeth right here," Dee recalled. "And she rubbed and she just said, 'Oh, that's so sweet. That's what a girlfriend would do.' And I said, 'Well, you have a new girlfriend.'"

That was on a Saturday. The following Monday was Memorial Day — the anniversary of Remington's death. Peters started texting Dee. She sent pictures. She sent videos. "She was a grieving mom," Dee said simply. "And I just was her friend."

Grief does not follow a schedule and it does not expire. It changes shape, goes underground, resurfaces in forms the griever doesn't always recognize. A woman who loses her only son at a public event, in front of tens of thousands of people, for reasons she cannot fully make sense of — that woman carries something that doesn't have a clean name. It is not just sadness. It is the specific, ragged disorientation of a loss that feels random and therefore meaningless, and the desperate human need to make it mean something.

In the years after Remington died, Peters would begin to suggest that his death was not an accident. When she was running for clerk in 2018, she called it an accident, repeatedly. But after her arrest, a new story emerged: that the FBI had been involved. That Remington had been targeted. That his death, like her prosecution, was part of something larger and more sinister than it appeared.

Sheila Rae, who worked alongside Peters and watched her closely, noted the shift. "When she was running, it was an accident," Rae said. "She would say over and over, my son died in an accident. But since she's gotten herself in trouble, the conspiracy is that the FBI has done it all."

The footlocker sat at the foot of her bed for years after Remington died — his things, returned to her by the military, unopened. She couldn't bring herself to look inside. When law enforcement searched her home in 2021, they went through it. Her supporters described agents scattering his belongings across the floor. Whether that is precisely what happened, or a story shaped by grief and fury into something sharper than the facts, is impossible to know from the outside.

What is not impossible to know is this: she had a son, and he fell out of the sky, and she never quite put that down.

iv. The Same Month

Remington Peters died on May 28, 2017. In June 2017 — the following month — Tina and Thomas Peters legally separated. They had been married for 36 years.

This detail has not received much attention in the coverage of Tina Peters. It should.

Tom Peters had Parkinson's disease and dementia. His condition had been deteriorating for years. The separation — and the complicated, painful legal proceedings that followed — was not a clean break. It was another kind of loss layered on top of the first: the slow erosion of a man she had known for most of her adult life, happening simultaneously with the sudden, violent absence of her only child.

Psychologists who study grief describe a phenomenon called bereavement overload — the compounding of losses so close together that the mind cannot process them sequentially and instead experiences them as a single, undifferentiated catastrophe. The normal work of mourning — which is already hard, which takes years even under the best circumstances — becomes nearly impossible when the losses pile up faster than they can be absorbed. What remains is not grief exactly, but something rawer: a permanent state of emergency, a sense that the world has become fundamentally unsafe, that the ground beneath ordinary life is not solid.

Two years later, Tina Peters ran for office for the first time.

The relationship between her personal losses and her political radicalization is not something she has ever discussed directly or analytically — she is not that kind of public figure. But the timing is worth sitting with. She lost her son in May 2017. She separated from her husband in June 2017. She was elected clerk in November 2018. She met Douglas Frank and Sherronna Bishop in April 2021. By August 2021, she was on a plane to Mike Lindell's symposium while her office was being raided.

That is four years from catastrophic personal loss to federal investigation. Four years during which she went from a woman nobody had heard of to a woman the Secretary of State of Colorado was trying to remove from office.

The legal proceedings around her divorce became, in Peters' telling, another front in the war being waged against her. She began telling audiences that Mesa County District Attorney Dan Rubinstein had gone to the nursing home where Tom was a patient and forced him, a man with advanced dementia, to sign divorce papers. Rubinstein denied this. Reporting found no evidence it was true. The story drew gasps at her rallies. Donations followed.

Tom Peters filed a civil lawsuit against her, alleging she had used a fraudulent Power of Attorney to transfer ownership of his home to herself — filing the deed in the very recording office she controlled as clerk. His family moved him out of Grand Junction. Tina sued them in return. The litigation continued for years.

Tom died on December 31, 2023, while Tina was preparing for trial.

On what would have been their 37th wedding anniversary, she posted to social media: "Today 1/11 my husband and I would've been married 37 years. Instead he died of neglect less than 2 weeks ago because of the criminal acts of those persecuting and prosecuting me for revealing their dirty secret to steal your vote."

Read that sentence slowly. She is saying that the people prosecuting her for election tampering killed her husband. She is saying his death — the death of a man with Parkinson's and dementia, who had been deteriorating for years — was their fault. She is saying everything is connected. The son, the husband, the prosecution, the conspiracy. One vast, coordinated attack on Tina Peters and the truth she carries.

This is what bereavement overload can do, in the wrong hands — not her hands specifically, but the hands of the people who found her in that state and gave her a story that made it all make sense. If everything that has happened to you is the result of a coordinated conspiracy, then nothing is random. Nothing is meaningless. Your son did not die for nothing. Your husband was not taken by a disease. You are not a woman to whom terrible things simply happened. You are a target. And targets matter.

v. The Conversion

A narrative in which she was not a struggling, grieving, embattled local official who couldn't pass her clerk certification. She was a guardian of democracy. Someone with unique access to prove a historic crime. Someone who mattered.

Explore conspiratorial thinking as identity and belonging. The research on how personal loss and social isolation can increase susceptibility to movements that offer purpose and community. This is not about making excuses — it's about understanding the mechanism.

vi. How She Sees Herself

YOUR TEXT GOES HERE. Draw on her own words across interviews, rallies, court appearances. She is remarkably consistent: whistleblower, not criminal. Gold Star mother targeted by a corrupt deep state. Faithful public servant trying to preserve records. A woman whose husband was stolen from her by a corrupt DA. At sentencing, she spoke for 40 minutes. She showed the judge photos of her son.

"I've never done anything with malice to break the law. I've only wanted to serve the people of Mesa County."

Tina Peters  ·  Sentencing hearing, October 3, 2024

She offered to stop talking about the election if the judge would give her probation. Was that pragmatic survival — or a rare moment of self-awareness? What does her self-narrative tell us about the psychology of true belief?

vii. The Admission

YOUR CLOSING GOES HERE. In May 2026, Peters reportedly told Governor Polis she had "made a mistake" and would follow the law — a statement that secured her release. At sentencing 18 months earlier, she was defiant and said she'd do it all again. What changed? Is the admission sincere, or strategic? Does the distinction matter? And what does it mean to understand someone fully — to see both the grief and the harm, the humanity and the damage?
