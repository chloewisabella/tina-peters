Who Is Tina Peters to Herself?

The grief, the losses, the identity she built from them — and the worldview that filled the gap. How personal trauma can lay the groundwork for radical belief.

~25 min read 7 sections
i. September 11, 1955

Tina Marie Peters was born on September 11, 1955. She grew up in the United States, in a family her supporters describe as having a strong sense of service. She became a flight attendant. She studied holistic nutrition through a correspondence course. She married a man named Tom Peters and helped, to varying degrees depending on who you ask, run his construction company in Grand Junction, Colorado. She raised a son. She lived, for most of her adult life, in the quiet middle of things — not obscure exactly, but not notable either.

There is a particular kind of ambition that doesn't announce itself until late — that waits, accumulates, and then arrives all at once. Peters was 63 years old when she first ran for public office. Most people, at 63, are winding down. She was just starting.

What drove her to run is a question that gets different answers depending on who you ask. She has said she wanted to improve service at the county's Department of Motor Vehicles — a mundane, local concern, the kind of thing that genuinely bothers people and that a county clerk can actually fix. Her supporters describe a woman who cared deeply about her community. Her critics describe a woman who wanted a platform and found one. Both may be true.

What is documented is the shape of her life before politics: unconventional, peripatetic, not easily categorized. Her degree in holistic nutrition came from Clayton College of Natural Health — a correspondence institution that was never accredited by a recognized accrediting body and has since closed. It is the kind of credential that signals something about a person's relationship to official knowledge — a preference for alternatives, a skepticism of gatekeepers, a belief that what the institutions offer isn't necessarily the whole picture. It does not make her unusual, exactly. But it is a data point.

She is not a stupid woman. Everyone who has spent time with her — supporters and critics alike — makes that clear. What she is, or became, is something harder to name: a person who found, late in life, a cause that made everything else make sense. The question this article tries to answer is how she got there.

iii. May 28, 2017

YOUR TEXT GOES HERE. This is the emotional center of the article. Remington J. Peters — her only son, a Navy SEAL — died on May 28, 2017, when his parachute failed to open during a public air show demonstration over the Hudson River in New York City. He was performing stunts: carrying a huge American flag, leaving colored smoke trails. He was wearing a wingsuit. His primary parachute lines became entangled.

The military ruled his death was in the line of duty. Peters began calling herself a Gold Star mother — a designation that technically requires combat death. She never corrected the impression. Her supporters say she was a grieving mother. Her critics say she exploited it.

His footlocker sat at the foot of her bed, unopened since it was returned to her after his death. She couldn't bring herself to open it. When law enforcement searched her home, they scattered its contents across the floor.

iv. The Same Month

YOUR TEXT GOES HERE. This is perhaps the most important detail in the entire project, and it has never been fully reported. In June 2017 — the same month Remington died — Peters legally separated from her husband Tom, who had Parkinson's disease and dementia. She lost her son and her 36-year marriage simultaneously. Two years later, she ran for office for the first time. What happens to a person in that gap?

Explore: the psychology of simultaneous catastrophic loss. The way grief can destabilize a person's relationship to truth, to community, to self. Not as diagnosis — as human context.

A narrative in which she was not a struggling, grieving, embattled local official who couldn't pass her clerk certification. She was a guardian of democracy. vi. How She Sees Herself

YOUR TEXT GOES HERE. Draw on her own words across interviews, rallies, court appearances. She is remarkably consistent: whistleblower, not criminal. Gold Star mother targeted by a corrupt deep state. Faithful public servant trying to preserve records. A woman whose husband was stolen from her by a corrupt DA. At sentencing, she spoke for 40 minutes. She showed the judge photos of her son.

"I've never done anything with malice to break the law. I've only wanted to serve the people of Mesa County."

Tina Peters  ·  Sentencing hearing, October 3, 2024

She offered to stop talking about the election if the judge would give her probation. Was that pragmatic survival — or a rare moment of self-awareness? What does her self-narrative tell us about the psychology of true belief?

vii. The Admission

YOUR CLOSING GOES HERE. In May 2026, Peters reportedly told Governor Polis she had "made a mistake" and would follow the law — a statement that secured her release. At sentencing 18 months earlier, she was defiant and said she'd do it all again. What changed? Is the admission sincere, or strategic? Does the distinction matter? And what does it mean to understand someone fully — to see both the grief and the harm, the humanity and the damage?
