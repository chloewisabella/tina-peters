Part II of III The Inner Life

Who Is Tina Peters to Herself?

The grief, the losses, the identity she built from them — and the worldview that filled the gap. How personal trauma can lay the groundwork for radical belief.

~25 min read 7 sections
i. September 11, 1955

Tina Marie Peters was born Sept. 11, 1955. Before she became the first election official in American history convicted of crimes tied to overturning a presidential election, she was a flight attendant, then a nutritionist, then a co-owner of a construction business. She ran for public office for the first time at 63. Peters holds a bachelor's degree in holistic nutrition from Clayton College of Natural Health, a non-accredited correspondence institution that closed in 2010. It is her only known academic credential. She never completed the certification required of Colorado county clerks. She campaigned for Mesa County Clerk in 2018 on a promise to reopen DMV satellite offices that had been shuttered by budget cuts. She defeated Bobbie Gross, a 10-year veteran of the clerk's office, in the Republican primary. Sheila Rae, the clerk who preceded Gross and held the position for eight years before term limits pushed her out, attempted to brief Peters before she took office. "It was really apparent right from the beginning that she wasn't very interested in how the office worked," Rae said. "How to do certain transactions, how to request a budget — none of that interested her." What happened over the next five years — the security breach, the indictment, the national profile, the nine-year prison sentence — has been documented extensively. Less examined is the question of how Peters got there. This article looks at the personal history behind the public record.

ii. The Outsider Credential

Clayton College of Natural Health, where Peters earned her bachelor's degree in holistic nutrition, was a correspondence institution — meaning coursework was completed by mail, with no campus attendance required. The school was never accredited by a nationally recognized accrediting body. It closed in 2010.

The degree was Peters' only known academic credential when she took office as Mesa County Clerk in 2019. Colorado does not require county clerks to hold a specific educational background — the position is elected, not appointed — but the state does require clerks to complete a certification program through the Colorado County Clerks Association.

Peters never completed it.

"She does not understand the job," Matt Crane, executive director of the Colorado County Clerks Association, told the court at Peters' sentencing. "She did not attempt to master it. She did not complete the work to be a certified election official."

Former Mesa County elections coordinator Karen Delgado, who worked in the clerk's office during Peters' tenure, said the gap between Peters' credentials and the demands of the job was apparent early on.

"Elections work is technical," Delgado said. "There are federal and state statutes, there are deadlines, there are security protocols. You have to know the systems. She wasn't interested in learning any of it."

Rae, who ran the office for eight years before Peters took over, said she attempted to ease the transition by including Peters in meetings and staff interviews before she officially took office.

"None of that interested her," Rae said.

What did interest her, according to people who worked alongside her, was the political dimension of the job. Peters attended Republican events, cultivated relationships with conservative activists and positioned herself as a voice for election integrity — a term that, in the post-2020 political environment, carried significant ideological weight.

By April 2021, that positioning would bring two visitors to her office who would change the course of her tenure entirely.

iii. May 28, 2017

On May 28, 2017, Petty Officer 1st Class Remington J. Peters jumped from a helicopter over the Hudson River in New York City as part of a Navy SEAL parachute demonstration team performing at a Memorial Day air show. His primary parachute lines became entangled. He fell. Peters was 923 jumps into his career. He was performing as part of the Navy's Leap Frogs demonstration team, carrying colored smoke and an American flag before a crowd of tens of thousands. The military ruled his death was in the line of duty. He did not die in combat. Under federal law, Gold Star family status requires a service member to have been killed in action against an enemy of the United States or while engaged in qualifying military operations abroad. Remington Peters' death did not meet those criteria. James Whitfield, a former Navy SEAL who served alongside demonstration teams during the same period, said accidents during air show performances, while rare, are a known risk of the work. "These guys are elite," Whitfield said. "But parachute malfunctions happen. It's equipment, it's physics. It doesn't mean someone did something wrong." Tina Peters began referring to herself publicly as a Gold Star mother following Remington's death. The designation has been repeated without qualification by her supporters and in right-wing media coverage of her case. Peters has not publicly corrected the characterization. At her sentencing hearing in October 2024, Peters displayed photographs of Remington to the court and cited his death as part of her argument for probation. "I'm not a criminal and I don't deserve to go into a prison where other people have committed heinous crimes," she told the judge. Dee Valdez Pepper, a Republican activist from Elbert County who became close with Peters in 2022, said she first connected with Peters the day after meeting her at a Lincoln Day dinner — on Memorial Day, the anniversary of Remington's death. "She started texting and sending pictures and videos," Valdez Pepper said. "She was a grieving mom. And I just was her friend." Peters' characterization of her son's death shifted after her arrest in 2022. During her 2018 campaign for clerk, she described it as an accident. After her indictment, she began suggesting the FBI was involved. "When she was running, it was an accident — she would say over and over, my son died in an accident," said Rae, the former Mesa County clerk. "But since she's gotten herself in trouble, the conspiracy is that the FBI has done it all." Sandra Oates, a Grand Junction resident who attended several of Peters' local appearances, said the shift in Peters' account of her son's death was noticeable. "The first time I heard her speak she talked about it like a tragedy," Oates said. "Later it became part of the whole story about how they were out to get her. It felt different." When law enforcement searched Peters' home following the 2021 breach, her supporters alleged that agents disturbed a footlocker belonging to Remington that had sat unopened at the foot of her bed since his death. The allegation became a rallying point for her supporters online and in right-wing media. Law enforcement did not publicly respond to the specific claim.

iv. The Same Month

Remington Peters died May 28, 2017. The following month, Tina and Thomas Peters legally separated. They had been married 36 years. Thomas Peters had Parkinson's disease and dementia. Court records show the couple filed a decree of legal separation in January 2018. In October 2021 — the same month Peters was suspended from her election duties by the Colorado Secretary of State — she filed two documents in the recording office she controlled: one revoking her power of attorney over Thomas Peters, and a quit-claim deed transferring ownership of a home he had purchased separately after their separation to herself. Thomas Peters subsequently filed a civil lawsuit against her. One of his attornies who is not authorized to speak on record said the filing was straightforward. "Mr. Peters purchased that property with his own funds after the separation," the attourney said. "The transfer was not authorized. We are seeking to have it returned." Peters denied wrongdoing. She told audiences at rallies and public appearances that Mesa County District Attorney Dan Rubinstein had gone to the nursing home where Thomas was a patient and forced him — a man with advanced dementia — to sign divorce papers. Rubinstein denied the account. Reporting found no evidence supporting Peters' version of events. Thomas Peters died Dec. 31, 2023, while Peters was preparing for trial. On what would have been their 37th wedding anniversary, Peters posted to social media: "Today 1/11 my husband and I would've been married 37 years. Instead he died of neglect less than 2 weeks ago because of the criminal acts of those persecuting and prosecuting me for revealing their dirty secret to steal your vote." Alan Wolfelts, a Fort Collins-based grief counselor who has worked with clients experiencing multiple compounding losses, said the pattern Peters described publicly is consistent with what mental health professionals call traumatic bereavement. "When someone experiences major losses in rapid succession — a child, a marriage, a sense of identity — the mind often looks for a framework to contain it," Wolfelt said. "Sometimes that framework is conspiratorial. It's more tolerable to believe someone caused your suffering than to accept that it was random." Peters has not discussed her mental health publicly. She has consistently maintained that her actions as clerk were motivated by a genuine belief that the 2020 election was compromised, and that her personal losses are evidence of a coordinated campaign against her.

v. The Conversion

On April 23, 2021, two visitors came to see Peters at her office in Grand Junction. Prosecutors would later call the meeting "the beginning of the conspiracy." The first visitor was Sherronna Bishop, a conservative activist who had managed Lauren Boebert's 2020 congressional campaign. The second was Douglas Frank, a former Ohio math teacher who had spent the months since the 2020 election traveling the country presenting debunked claims of voter fraud. Frank was paid by MyPillow CEO Mike Lindell to make those trips. A secret recording of the meeting, played at Peters' trial, captured Frank telling those present: "I would be glad to help you find phantom ballots. I've been going around the country helping lots of people, and Mike Lindell pays me to do that." Peters had signed documents the previous fall certifying that Mesa County's 2020 election results contained no discrepancies. After the April 23 meeting, she did not make that claim publicly again. Rachel Nielson, a political radicalization researcher at the University of Denver, said the sequence of events in Peters' case follows a documented pattern. "You often see individuals who are experiencing personal instability — job pressures, family loss, social isolation — become more susceptible to movements that offer a clear narrative and a community," Nielson said. "The movement doesn't create the vulnerability. It finds it." Prosecutors said at trial that Peters had become "fixated" on voting problems after falling in with the election-denial movement and was "seeking fame." Dee Valdez Pepper, a Republican activist from Elbert County who became one of Peters' closest supporters, said she did not know Peters' full story when they first met at a Lincoln Day dinner in 2022. "I didn't really know the story prior to that," Valdez Pepper said. "I knew a little bit of it, but not a lot. And that's really how we became friends — when I became a supporter." Valdez Pepper now runs a weekly prayer call for Peters every Thursday at 12:10 p.m. She said participation regularly draws people from more than a dozen states. "She's a very strong woman of God," Valdez Pepper said. "She has more faith than anyone I've ever seen in my life."

vi. How She Sees Herself

Throughout her legal proceedings, Peters maintained a consistent public account of her own motivations. She did not testify at her criminal trial. But across rallies, podcasts, court statements and social media posts, she described herself in largely the same terms: a public servant who discovered evidence of election fraud, reported it through proper channels, was ignored and ultimately took matters into her own hands. "I've never done anything with malice to break the law," Peters told the court at her October 2024 sentencing. "I've only wanted to serve the people of Mesa County." She described herself repeatedly as a whistleblower — a characterization her legal team advanced and her supporters adopted. In appearances on right-wing media, she framed her prosecution as a coordinated effort to silence election integrity advocates nationwide. "What can happen to Tina can happen to any of us," Valdez Pepper said. "And that's why her case is so important." Pastor Greg Holloway, who leads a congregation in Grand Junction and has prayed publicly for Peters, said her faith has been central to how she has framed her experience. "She sees this as a calling," Holloway said. "In her mind, she was put in that position for a reason, and what happened to her is part of something larger. That's how she makes sense of it." Not everyone who initially supported Peters maintained that view. Mark Tenney, a Mesa County Republican who attended several of her early appearances, said his opinion of her shifted as the legal case developed. "At first I thought she was brave," Tenney said. "But when the details came out — the fake credentials, the guy she smuggled in — it didn't add up with the story she was telling. She wasn't a whistleblower. She ran an operation." Peters' brother-in-law, Jack Peters, who described himself to the Grand Junction Daily Sentinel as "extremely conservative," said Peters was "unfairly using her former husband and her deceased son to gain sympathy in her criminal case." "I've always been a bit at odds with Tina," Jack Peters told the Sentinel. "She's always been somewhat abrasive." At her sentencing, Peters spoke for 40 minutes. She displayed photographs of Remington and Thomas Peters. She asked for probation. She told the judge that if he ordered her not to talk about elections, she would comply. Judge Matthew Barrett denied probation and sentenced her to nine years.

vii. The Admission

In May 2026, Peters submitted a clemency application to Colorado Governor Jared Polis. According to CNN, Peters admitted in the application that she had made a mistake and stated she would follow the law going forward. It was a departure from the position she had held publicly for five years. At her sentencing 18 months earlier, she had told the court she had done nothing wrong. She had continued making public appearances from prison, calling into a Colorado GOP fundraising dinner hosted by Steve Bannon to maintain her public profile. Polis granted the commutation May 15, 2026, making Peters eligible for parole June 1. "She committed a crime," Polis said. "She deserves to be a convicted felon. She deserves to do the time. But she has unpopular, and in my opinion, incorrect opinions. Those should not be a factor in her sentencing." Valdez Pepper said she believes the admission was required by circumstance, not conviction. "She said what she needed to say to get out," Valdez Pepper said. "That doesn't mean she's changed her mind about any of it." Peters has not made public statements since the commutation was granted. She is expected to be released on parole in June 2026. Her criminal conviction stands.
