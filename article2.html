Who Is Tina Peters to Herself?

The grief, the losses, the identity she built from them — and the worldview that filled the gap. How personal trauma can lay the groundwork for radical belief.

~25 min read 7 sections
i. September 11, 1955

Tina Marie Peters was born on September 11, 1955. She grew up in the United States, in a family her supporters describe as having a strong sense of service. She became a flight attendant. She studied holistic nutrition through a correspondence course. She married a man named Tom Peters and helped, to varying degrees depending on who you ask, run his construction company in Grand Junction, Colorado. She raised a son. She lived, for most of her adult life, in the quiet middle of things — not obscure exactly, but not notable either.

There is a particular kind of ambition that doesn't announce itself until late — that waits, accumulates, and then arrives all at once. Peters was 63 years old when she first ran for public office. Most people, at 63, are winding down. She was just starting.

What drove her to run is a question that gets different answers depending on who you ask. She has said she wanted to improve service at the county's Department of Motor Vehicles — a mundane, local concern, the kind of thing that genuinely bothers people and that a county clerk can actually fix. Her supporters describe a woman who cared deeply about her community. Her critics describe a woman who wanted a platform and found one. Both may be true.

What is documented is the shape of her life before politics: unconventional, peripatetic, not easily categorized. Her degree in holistic nutrition came from Clayton College of Natural Health — a correspondence institution that was never accredited by a recognized accrediting body and has since closed. It is the kind of credential that signals something about a person's relationship to official knowledge — a preference for alternatives, a skepticism of gatekeepers, a belief that what the institutions offer isn't necessarily the whole picture. It does not make her unusual, exactly. But it is a data point.

She is not a stupid woman. Everyone who has spent time with her — supporters and critics alike — makes that clear. What she is, or became, is something harder to name: a person who found, late in life, a cause that made everything else make sense. The question this article tries to answer is how she got there.

ii. The Outsider Credential

Clayton College of Natural Health was founded in 1980 in Birmingham, Alabama. It offered degrees in holistic health, natural nutrition, and alternative medicine through correspondence — students received materials by mail, completed coursework at home, and were awarded credentials without ever setting foot in a classroom or being evaluated by an accredited examining body. The school closed in 2010, after decades of operating outside the mainstream of American higher education. Its degrees were not recognized by the major accrediting bodies that govern academic institutions in the United States.

Tina Peters holds a bachelor's degree from Clayton College in holistic nutrition. It is her only known academic credential.

This is not, by itself, disqualifying. County clerks are not required to have particular educational backgrounds — they are elected officials, accountable to voters, not to licensing boards. Plenty of people who hold alternative or non-traditional credentials are thoughtful, capable, and honest. The credential itself is not the point.

The point is what it signals about a person's relationship to institutions. Clayton College existed in explicit opposition to mainstream medicine and mainstream academia — it offered an alternative to what it characterized as a gatekept, profit-driven, corrupt official knowledge system. To choose it, rather than an accredited school, is to make a statement: the official version isn't the whole story. There is knowledge the gatekeepers don't want you to have.

That same logic — the official version is incomplete, the gatekeepers are hiding something, the truth requires going outside the established channels — runs directly through everything Peters did in 2021. It is the logic of the April 23 meeting, of the trusted build breach, of the Selection Code documentary. It is the logic of the election-denial movement itself.

The research on radicalization suggests that people who feel excluded from credentialed institutions are more susceptible to alternative knowledge systems that validate their existing suspicions. Peters was not excluded from credentialed institutions in any formal sense — she chose, repeatedly, to operate outside them. She chose a correspondence school over an accredited university. She chose to run for clerk without completing the certification the job required. She chose to bring in outside experts — Frank, Hayes — rather than work within the state's established election infrastructure.

None of this proves anything. But the pattern is consistent. Tina Peters has spent most of her adult life operating in the margins of official systems, convinced that the margins are where the real work gets done.

She was not wrong about everything. Sometimes the margins are exactly that.

iii. May 28, 2017

On May 28, 2017, Remington J. Peters jumped out of a helicopter over the Hudson River in New York City. He was a member of the U.S. Navy SEAL parachute demonstration team — the Leap Frogs — and that afternoon they were performing for a crowd of tens of thousands who had gathered for a Memorial Day air show. He was wearing a wingsuit. He was carrying colored smoke. He was 923 jumps into a career that had taken him to the most elite corners of the American military.

His primary parachute lines became entangled. He fell.

The military ruled his death was in the line of duty. He was not, technically, in combat. He was performing stunts over a river in front of a crowd, on a holiday weekend, for reasons that had everything to do with pageantry and nothing to do with war. A woman in the crowd told the New York Times it was "so unnecessary, just to entertain people, for someone to have died." She was not wrong, exactly. She was also not the one who had lost a son.

Tina Peters began calling herself a Gold Star mother. The Gold Star designation formally applies to families of service members killed in combat — in action against an enemy, on foreign soil as part of a peacekeeping force, or under other congressionally defined criteria. Remington's death, ruled as it was, did not meet those criteria. Peters does not appear to have corrected this impression when it arose in public. Her supporters call her a Gold Star mother without qualification. She has let them.

What she has done with her grief is a question that divides people who know her. Her critics say she weaponized it — that Remington became a rhetorical device, a way of generating sympathy and donations that had nothing to do with his memory and everything to do with her legal defense. Her supporters say she is a mother who lost her only son and has never fully recovered, and that to question how she mourns is indecent.

Both of these things can be true at once.

Dee Valdez Pepper met Tina Peters at a Lincoln Day dinner in Elbert County in 2022 — a small, rural Republican county between Colorado Springs and Denver. Peters was campaigning for Secretary of State. She was standing in the back of the room when Dee noticed she had lipstick on her teeth. "I just said, oh, you got a little bit of lipstick on your teeth right here," Dee recalled. "And she rubbed and she just said, 'Oh, that's so sweet. That's what a girlfriend would do.' And I said, 'Well, you have a new girlfriend.'"

That was on a Saturday. The following Monday was Memorial Day — the anniversary of Remington's death. Peters started texting Dee. She sent pictures. She sent videos. "She was a grieving mom," Dee said simply. "And I just was her friend."

Grief does not follow a schedule and it does not expire. It changes shape, goes underground, resurfaces in forms the griever doesn't always recognize. A woman who loses her only son at a public event, in front of tens of thousands of people, for reasons she cannot fully make sense of — that woman carries something that doesn't have a clean name. It is not just sadness. It is the specific, ragged disorientation of a loss that feels random and therefore meaningless, and the desperate human need to make it mean something.

In the years after Remington died, Peters would begin to suggest that his death was not an accident. When she was running for clerk in 2018, she called it an accident, repeatedly. But after her arrest, a new story emerged: that the FBI had been involved. That Remington had been targeted. That his death, like her prosecution, was part of something larger and more sinister than it appeared.

Sheila Rae, who worked alongside Peters and watched her closely, noted the shift. "When she was running, it was an accident," Rae said. "She would say over and over, my son died in an accident. But since she's gotten herself in trouble, the conspiracy is that the FBI has done it all."

The footlocker sat at the foot of her bed for years after Remington died — his things, returned to her by the military, unopened. She couldn't bring herself to look inside. When law enforcement searched her home in 2021, they went through it. Her supporters described agents scattering his belongings across the floor. Whether that is precisely what happened, or a story shaped by grief and fury into something sharper than the facts, is impossible to know from the outside.

What is not impossible to know is this: she had a son, and he fell out of the sky, and she never quite put that down.

Faithful public servant trying to preserve records. A woman whose husband was stolen from her by a corrupt DA. At sentencing, she spoke for 40 minutes. She showed the judge photos of her son.

She offered to stop talking about the election if the judge would give her probation. Was that pragmatic survival — or a rare moment of self-awareness? What does her self-narrative tell us about the psychology of true belief?
