Who Is Tina Peters?

She was a county clerk in a quiet Colorado town. Then she became the first election official in American history convicted of crimes tied to overturning a presidential election. This is the full story.

Updated May 2026 ~25 min read 9 sections
i. A Quiet County

Grand Junction sits at the western edge of Colorado, where the Rockies flatten out into red rock and high desert. The Colorado and Gunnison rivers meet here, cutting through canyon country on their way toward Utah. It is peach orchard country, oil and gas country, a place where pickup trucks outnumber Priuses by a ratio that feels almost philosophical. Mesa County votes Republican by margins that would embarrass most swing states — in 2020, Donald Trump carried it by 40 points. It is not a place where people expect to make national news.

Tina Marie Peters was not a person who expected to make national news, either. She was 63 when she first ran for office in 2018, a former flight attendant who had spent years helping manage her husband's construction business. Her degree was in holistic nutrition, from a correspondence school that has since closed. She had never held elected office. She had never worked a day in the Mesa County Clerk's office. She ran on a platform of reopening DMV satellite offices that had been shuttered by budget cuts.

She won.

Her opponent, Bobbie Gross, had worked in the clerk's office for ten years. Sheila Rae, the clerk who had held the position before Gross, tried to bring Peters into the fold before she officially took office — inviting her to meetings, including her in interviews for staff who would ultimately work for her. "It was really apparent right from the beginning," Rae recalled, "that she wasn't very interested in how the office worked."

The DMV offices reopened. By most measures, that was the high point.

In February 2020, workers discovered a ballot drop box sitting right outside the front entrance of the Mesa County elections headquarters. Inside were 574 uncounted ballots from the November 2019 election — ballots that had been sitting there, in a box, in a parking lot, for months. Peters attributed it to human error. Her response to the public, according to Rae, was essentially: it doesn't matter, the outcome wouldn't have changed anyway.

A group of residents didn't see it that way. They launched a recall effort. It was 2020 — COVID was spreading, and gathering signatures meant handing a pen to a stranger. They fell about 2,000 signatures short of the 12,000 needed. The recall failed.

Nobody could have known, then, what was coming. She was a county clerk who had lost some ballots and shrugged. She was not yet a martyr, a villain, or a cautionary tale. She was just a woman who had gotten a job she wasn't quite ready for, in a county that hadn't found a reason to look too closely.

That was about to change.

ii. The Job She Wasn't Ready For

The Mesa County Clerk and Recorder's office is not a glamorous posting. It handles voter registration, property records, motor vehicle titles, and marriage licenses. It is, by design, invisible — the kind of government office that only makes news when something goes wrong. For most of its history, nothing went wrong.

Sheila Rae ran the office for eight years before term limits pushed her out. She had started there 24 years earlier, maintaining voter registration rolls, and worked her way up. When Peters won the 2018 primary, Rae did what outgoing officials are supposed to do: she tried to help. She invited Peters to meetings, included her in staff interviews, tried to hand off institutional knowledge to the woman who would take her place.

It didn't take. "She wasn't very interested in how the office worked," Rae recalled. "How to do certain transactions, how to request a budget — none of that interested her." The people Rae had hired, the systems she had built, the relationships she had cultivated over two decades — Peters arrived and looked past all of it.

The trouble started almost immediately. In November 2019, ballots from a municipal election were placed in a drop box right outside the front doors of the elections office. No one collected them. They sat there through the winter. They were still there the following February, when staff preparing for the presidential primary stumbled across them — 574 votes, uncounted, in a box in a parking lot. Peters called it human error. When pressed by the public, she offered what Rae described as a shrug: even if they'd been counted, they wouldn't have changed the outcome.

For a group of Mesa County residents, that wasn't good enough. They launched a recall campaign in the spring of 2020, gathering signatures to force a public vote on whether Peters should keep her job. The effort was serious — they needed 12,000 signatures, and they got within 2,000 of that number. But it was 2020. A pandemic was spreading. Handing a pen to a stranger felt different that year. The recall fell short.

"Out of 30 some people that worked for me when I left," Rae said, "there were three people left" by the time Peters was removed from office. The turnover alone — good people who had shown up and done their jobs for years — was its own kind of damage, largely invisible to anyone outside the building.

Matt Crane, executive director of the Colorado County Clerks Association, would later tell a courtroom that Peters "did not understand the job, did not attempt to master it, did not complete the work to be a certified election official." He was not wrong. Peters never finished the certification required of county clerks in Colorado. The judge who would eventually sentence her made the same observation from the bench.

What Peters did have was a growing conviction that the system around her was broken — and that she was the only one paying attention. That conviction, still unfocused in 2020, was about to find its shape.

iii. April 23, 2021

On April 23, 2021, two visitors came to see Tina Peters at her office in Grand Junction. Prosecutors would later call what happened that afternoon "the beginning of the conspiracy." At the time, it probably felt like a conversation.

The first visitor was Sherronna Bishop, a makeup artist and activist who had spent the previous year as Lauren Boebert's congressional campaign manager. Bishop moved in the orbit of the national election-denial movement — the loose constellation of podcasters, mathematicians, and true believers who had spent the months since November 2020 insisting, against all evidence, that something had gone wrong. She had a gift for connecting people. That afternoon, she connected Peters to the second visitor.

His name was Douglas Frank. He was a former math teacher from Ohio who had reinvented himself, in the months after the 2020 election, as a kind of roving prophet of election fraud — traveling from county to county, state to state, presenting charts and equations that he claimed proved the vote had been manipulated. His claims had been debunked by mathematicians and election officials alike. He kept traveling anyway.

The meeting took place in a conference room at the Mesa County Clerk's office — fluorescent lights, folding chairs, the kind of room where nothing important is supposed to happen.

A secret recording of the meeting was later played at Peters' trial. On it, Frank can be heard telling the room: "I would be glad to help you find phantom ballots. I've been going around the country helping lots of people, and Mike Lindell pays me to do that."

Mike Lindell. The MyPillow CEO had become, in the year since the election, the most prolific funder of election-denial activism in America. He was paying Frank to travel. He was paying for symposiums, documentaries, legal fees. He was building, piece by piece, a movement — and he needed local officials, people with access to the actual machines, to be part of it. Peters, embattled and overlooked, was exactly the kind of person the movement was looking for.

What the movement offered her in return was harder to quantify. It wasn't just a theory. It was a role. In the story Frank and Bishop were telling, Peters wasn't a struggling county clerk who had lost a box of ballots and nearly been recalled. She was a guardian. A witness. Someone with unique access to the truth that powerful people were trying to suppress. Someone who mattered.

Peters had signed documents the previous fall certifying that Mesa County's 2020 election results contained no discrepancies. She believed that, when she signed. After April 23, she would never say so publicly again.

The following month, a routine software update of the county's Dominion voting machines was scheduled — what election officials call a "trusted build." Peters began making plans.

iv. The Trusted Build

To get an outsider into a secure government facility without authorization, you need a name. You need a badge. You need a reason for that person to be there that nobody will question. Tina Peters spent the weeks after April 23 working out the details.

The trusted build — the software update of Mesa County's Dominion voting machines — was scheduled for May 2021. Under normal circumstances, it was a routine procedure: state-certified technicians would come in, update the system, and leave. Observers were permitted, but they had to be vetted. Peters needed someone inside who wasn't supposed to be there.

She found her outsider in Conan James Hayes, a retired surfer from California with no official role in Mesa County government. Hayes had connections to Mike Lindell's orbit. He also had, investigators would later discover, connections to a similar breach of voting equipment in Coffee County, Georgia — suggesting that what happened in Mesa County was not improvised, but part of something larger and more coordinated.

The problem was getting Hayes in. The trusted build required security credentials. Peters solved this by creating a paper identity for a local man named Gerald Wood, whom she was pretending to hire as a computer consultant for the elections office. She told different people different things about who Wood was — a state employee to some, a contractor to others, a new hire in the motor vehicle division to others still. She obtained credentials for Wood by skipping the normal vetting steps.

Then she used Wood's credentials to get Hayes in instead.

He walked in like he belonged there, which, on paper, he did. During the trusted build, while certified technicians performed the update they were authorized to perform, Hayes was in the room. He made copies of the hard drives of Mesa County's voting machines — sensitive election data, passwords, system records. He walked out with them.

In May 2021, Peters ordered the surveillance cameras monitoring the voting machines in the clerk's office turned off. She said they would be reactivated before the next election.

The copied data ended up online. Colorado Secretary of State Jena Griswold discovered the breach and launched an investigation. The FBI arrived at the Mesa County Clerk's office. Staff were interviewed. Equipment was quarantined. "The FBI doesn't know if staff's in on it," Sheila Rae recalled of those first chaotic days. "Who they could trust to stay in the office. It was a lot of pressure."

Peters, meanwhile, was on a plane to South Dakota. Mike Lindell was holding a "cyber symposium," and she was going to be there. Her office was being raided while she flew.

She came back to a county that would never quite be the same. The voting equipment was decertified and had to be replaced. Staff who had done nothing wrong were under suspicion. The community that had elected her was, in the words of one county commissioner, "a national laughingstock."

Peters didn't see it that way. She had the data. She had the proof. All she needed now was an audience.

Bobbie Gross, who now holds Peters' old job, is still rebuilding trust with 120,000 voters one conversation at a time.
