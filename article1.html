Who Is Tina Peters?

She was a county clerk in a quiet Colorado town. Then she became the first election official in American history convicted of crimes tied to overturning a presidential election. This is the full story.

Updated May 2026 ~25 min read 9 sections
i. A Quiet County

Grand Junction sits at the western edge of Colorado, where the Rockies flatten out into red rock and high desert. The Colorado and Gunnison rivers meet here, cutting through canyon country on their way toward Utah. It is peach orchard country, oil and gas country, a place where pickup trucks outnumber Priuses by a ratio that feels almost philosophical. Mesa County votes Republican by margins that would embarrass most swing states — in 2020, Donald Trump carried it by 40 points. It is not a place where people expect to make national news.

Tina Marie Peters was not a person who expected to make national news, either. She was 63 when she first ran for office in 2018, a former flight attendant who had spent years helping manage her husband's construction business. Her degree was in holistic nutrition, from a correspondence school that has since closed. She had never held elected office. She had never worked a day in the Mesa County Clerk's office. She ran on a platform of reopening DMV satellite offices that had been shuttered by budget cuts.

She won.

Her opponent, Bobbie Gross, had worked in the clerk's office for ten years. Sheila Rae, the clerk who had held the position before Gross, tried to bring Peters into the fold before she officially took office — inviting her to meetings, including her in interviews for staff who would ultimately work for her. "It was really apparent right from the beginning," Rae recalled, "that she wasn't very interested in how the office worked."

The DMV offices reopened. By most measures, that was the high point.

In February 2020, workers discovered a ballot drop box sitting right outside the front entrance of the Mesa County elections headquarters. Inside were 574 uncounted ballots from the November 2019 election — ballots that had been sitting there, in a box, in a parking lot, for months. Peters attributed it to human error. Her response to the public, according to Rae, was essentially: it doesn't matter, the outcome wouldn't have changed anyway.

A group of residents didn't see it that way. They launched a recall effort. It was 2020 — COVID was spreading, and gathering signatures meant handing a pen to a stranger. They fell about 2,000 signatures short of the 12,000 needed. The recall failed.

Nobody could have known, then, what was coming. She was a county clerk who had lost some ballots and shrugged. She was not yet a martyr, a villain, or a cautionary tale. She was just a woman who had gotten a job she wasn't quite ready for, in a county that hadn't found a reason to look too closely.

That was about to change.

She won the 2018 Republican primary for Mesa County Clerk and Recorder with 53% of the vote. Her opponent had worked in the clerk's office for ten years. Peters had never worked there a day in her life.

ii. The Job She Wasn't Ready For

The Mesa County Clerk and Recorder's office is not a glamorous posting. It handles voter registration, property records, motor vehicle titles, and marriage licenses. It is, by design, invisible — the kind of government office that only makes news when something goes wrong. For most of its history, nothing went wrong.

Sheila Rae ran the office for eight years before term limits pushed her out. She had started there 24 years earlier, maintaining voter registration rolls, and worked her way up. When Peters won the 2018 primary, Rae did what outgoing officials are supposed to do: she tried to help. She invited Peters to meetings, included her in staff interviews, tried to hand off institutional knowledge to the woman who would take her place.

It didn't take. "She wasn't very interested in how the office worked," Rae recalled. "How to do certain transactions, how to request a budget — none of that interested her." The people Rae had hired, the systems she had built, the relationships she had cultivated over two decades — Peters arrived and looked past all of it.

The trouble started almost immediately. In November 2019, ballots from a municipal election were placed in a drop box right outside the front doors of the elections office. No one collected them. They sat there through the winter. They were still there the following February, when staff preparing for the presidential primary stumbled across them — 574 votes, uncounted, in a box in a parking lot. Peters called it human error. When pressed by the public, she offered what Rae described as a shrug: even if they'd been counted, they wouldn't have changed the outcome.

For a group of Mesa County residents, that wasn't good enough. They launched a recall campaign in the spring of 2020, gathering signatures to force a public vote on whether Peters should keep her job. The effort was serious — they needed 12,000 signatures, and they got within 2,000 of that number. But it was 2020. A pandemic was spreading. Handing a pen to a stranger felt different that year. The recall fell short.

"Out of 30 some people that worked for me when I left," Rae said, "there were three people left" by the time Peters was removed from office. The turnover alone — good people who had shown up and done their jobs for years — was its own kind of damage, largely invisible to anyone outside the building.

Matt Crane, executive director of the Colorado County Clerks Association, would later tell a courtroom that Peters "did not understand the job, did not attempt to master it, did not complete the work to be a certified election official." He was not wrong. Peters never finished the certification required of county clerks in Colorado. The judge who would eventually sentence her made the same observation from the bench.

What Peters did have was a growing conviction that the system around her was broken — and that she was the only one paying attention. That conviction, still unfocused in 2020, was about to find its shape.
