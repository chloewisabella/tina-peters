Who Is Tina Peters?

She was a county clerk in a quiet Colorado town. Then she became the first election official in American history convicted of crimes tied to overturning a presidential election. This is the full story.

Updated May 2026 ~25 min read 9 sections
i. A Quiet County

Grand Junction sits at the western edge of Colorado, where high deserts meet the Gunnison river to cut through canyon country on its way to Utah. Mesa County, Colorado's western voting district, votes Republican by margins that would embarrass most swing states. In 2020, President Donald Trump carried it by 40 points. A quaint pocket of red tucked behind the dominant urban blue, Mesa is not a place where people expect to make national news.

Tina Marie Peters was not a person who expected to make national news, either. She was 63 when she first ran for office in 2018, a former flight attendant who had spent years helping manage her husband's construction business. Her degree was in holistic nutrition, from a correspondence school that has since closed. She had never held elected office. She had never worked a day in the Mesa County Clerk's office. She ran on a platform of reopening DMV satellite offices that had been shuttered by budget cuts.

She won.

Her opponent, Bobbie Gross, had worked in the clerk's office for ten years. Sheila Rae, the clerk who had held the position before Gross, tried to bring Peters into the fold before she officially took office — inviting her to meetings, including her in interviews for staff who would ultimately work for her.

"It was really apparent right from the beginning," Rae recalled, "that she wasn't very interested in how the office worked."

The DMV offices reopened. By most measures, that was the high point.

In February 2020, workers discovered a ballot drop box sitting right outside the front entrance of the Mesa County elections headquarters. Inside were 574 uncounted ballots from the November 2019 election — ballots that had been sitting there, in a box, in a parking lot, for months. Peters attributed it to human error. Her response to the public, according to Rae, was essentially: it doesn't matter, the outcome wouldn't have changed anyway.

A group of residents didn't see it that way. They launched a recall effort. It was 2020 — COVID was spreading, and gathering signatures meant handing a pen to a stranger. They fell about 2,000 signatures short of the 12,000 needed. The recall failed.

The recall failed. Peters kept her job. Seven months later, she would sign documents certifying that Mesa County's 2020 presidential election results contained no discrepancies.

ii. The Job She Wasn't Ready For

The Mesa County Clerk and Recorder's office handles voter registration, property records, motor vehicle titles and marriage licenses. Peters took office in January 2019.

Sheila Rae ran the office for eight years before term limits pushed her out. She had started there 24 years earlier, maintaining voter registration rolls, and worked her way up. When Peters won the 2018 primary, Rae attempted to brief Peters before she took office. She invited Peters to meetings, included her in staff interviews, tried to hand off institutional knowledge to the woman who would take her place. It didn't take.

"She wasn't very interested in how the office worked," Rae recalled. "How to do certain transactions, how to request a budget — none of that interested her."

The trouble started almost immediately. In November 2019, ballots from a municipal election were placed in a drop box right outside the front doors of the elections office. No one collected them. They sat there through the winter. They were still there the following February, when staff preparing for the presidential primary stumbled across them — 574 votes, uncounted, in a box in a parking lot. Peters called it human error. When pressed by the public, she offered what Rae described as a shrug: even if they'd been counted, they wouldn't have changed the outcome.

For a group of Mesa County residents, that wasn't good enough. They launched a recall campaign in the spring of 2020, gathering signatures to force a public vote on whether Peters should keep her job. The effort was serious — they needed 12,000 signatures, and they got within 2,000 of that number. But it was 2020. A pandemic was spreading. Handing a pen to a stranger felt different that year. The recall fell short.

"Out of 30 some people that worked for me when I left," Rae said, "there were three people left" by the time Peters was removed from office. The turnover alone — good people who had shown up and done their jobs for years — was its own kind of damage, largely invisible to anyone outside the building.

Matt Crane, executive director of the Colorado County Clerks Association, would later tell a courtroom that Peters "did not understand the job, did not attempt to master it, did not complete the work to be a certified election official."

Peters never finished the certification required of county clerks in Colorado. The judge who would eventually sentence her made the same observation from the bench

iii. The Beginning of the Conspiracy

On April 23, 2021, two visitors came to see Tina Peters at her office in Grand Junction. Prosecutors would later call what happened that afternoon "the beginning of the conspiracy."

The first visitor was Sherronna Bishop, a makeup artist and activist who had spent the previous year as Lauren Boebert's congressional campaign manager.

Bishop had managed Lauren Boebert's 2020 congressional campaign and had ties to national election-denial activists.

That afternoon, she connected Peters to the second visitor.

His name was Douglas Frank.

Frank was a former Ohio math teacher who had spent the months since the 2020 election traveling the country presenting debunked claims of voter fraud. He was paid by MyPillow CEO Mike Lindell to make those trips.

His claims had been debunked by mathematicians and election officials alike. He kept traveling anyway.

A secret recording of the meeting was later played at Peters' trial. On it, Frank can be heard telling the room: "I would be glad to help you find phantom ballots. I've been going around the country helping lots of people, and Mike Lindell pays me to do that."

Lindell had become one of the most prominent funders of election-denial activism in the country, bankrolling travel, legal fees and media productions for activists who shared his claims.

Prosecutors said Peters became "fixated" on voting problems after the meeting and began planning to bring an unauthorized person into the county's election equipment update the following month.

Peters had signed documents the previous fall certifying that Mesa County's 2020 election results contained no discrepancies. After April 23, she did not make that claim publicly again.

The following month, a routine software update of the county's Dominion voting machines was scheduled — what election officials call a "trusted build." Peters began making plans.

iv. The Trusted Build

In the weeks following the April 23 meeting, Peters began arranging unauthorized access to Mesa County's Dominion voting machine software update, scheduled for May 2021.

The trusted build — the software update of Mesa County's Dominion voting machines — was scheduled for May 2021. Under normal circumstances, it was a routine procedure: state-certified technicians would come in, update the system, and leave. Observers were permitted, but they had to be vetted. Peters needed someone inside who wasn't supposed to be there.

She found her outsider in Conan James Hayes, a retired surfer from California with no official role in Mesa County government. Hayes had connections to Mike Lindell's orbit. He also had, investigators would later discover, connections to a similar breach of voting equipment in Coffee County, Georgia — suggesting that what happened in Mesa County was not improvised, but part of something larger and more coordinated.

The problem was getting Hayes in. The trusted build required security credentials. Peters solved this by creating a paper identity for a local man named Gerald Wood, whom she was pretending to hire as a computer consultant for the elections office. She told different people different things about who Wood was — a state employee to some, a contractor to others, a new hire in the motor vehicle division to others still. She obtained credentials for Wood by skipping the normal vetting steps.

Then she used Wood's credentials to get Hayes in instead.

Hayes entered the facility using Wood's credentials. During the trusted build, while certified technicians performed the update they were authorized to perform, Hayes was in the room. He made copies of the hard drives of Mesa County's voting machines — sensitive election data, passwords, system records. He walked out with them.

In May 2021, Peters ordered the surveillance cameras monitoring the voting machines in the clerk's office turned off. She said they would be reactivated before the next election.

The copied data ended up online. Colorado Secretary of State Jena Griswold discovered the breach and launched an investigation. The FBI arrived at the Mesa County Clerk's office. Staff were interviewed. Equipment was quarantined. "The FBI doesn't know if staff's in on it," Sheila Rae recalled of those first chaotic days. "Who they could trust to stay in the office. It was a lot of pressure."

Peters, meanwhile, was on a plane to South Dakota. Mike Lindell was holding a "cyber symposium," and she was going to be there. Her office was being raided while she flew.

She came back to a county that would never quite be the same. The voting equipment was decertified and had to be replaced. Staff who had done nothing wrong were under suspicion. The community that had elected her was, in the words of one county commissioner, "a national laughingstock."

v. The Circus

Following the breach, Peters became a prominent figure in the national election-denial movement.

She appeared at rallies. She gave interviews. She became a fixture on the podcasts and livestreams of the election-denial movement — a local official with receipts, or so the story went. Mike Lindell produced a documentary about her called Selection Code, which cast her as the protagonist of a global conspiracy thriller — a humble county clerk who had stumbled onto the crime of the century. The film did not name Conan Hayes. It did not mention that the data had been copied and leaked. It presented Peters as a whistleblower and a martyr, and it found an audience.

In January 2022, she filed for reelection as Mesa County Clerk. A month later, she dropped that bid and announced she was running for Colorado Secretary of State instead — the statewide official who oversees all elections in Colorado.

She lost. She came in second out of three candidates in the Republican primary, finishing with 29 percent of the vote. She said she didn't accept the result. She suggested fraud.

"Half this county thinks she's a hero," said Grand Junction resident Matt Etheim. "The other half just wants their DMV back."

She paid $256,000 for a manual recount. The recount gave her 13 additional votes. She still had 29 percent. She still lost.

Through all of it, the legal machinery was grinding forward. Her deputy clerks, Belinda Knisley and Sandra Brown, were charged alongside her. Both eventually took plea deals in exchange for testifying against Peters. Knisley pleaded guilty to misdemeanors and was sentenced to community service. Brown pleaded guilty to a felony, with the agreement that the conviction would be erased after two years if she cooperated fully.

Peters pleaded not guilty to everything.

In October 2023, the Colorado Office of Administrative Courts fined her $15,400 for campaign finance violations — failing to register as a candidate, failing to file accurate donation and expenditure reports. It was a misdemeanor of the political variety, almost a footnote by that point.

The trial was set for the summer of 2024. By then, Peters had been indicted, fined, investigated by the FBI, removed from her position, beaten in a primary she called rigged, and made into a documentary subject. She had also, in that same period, become something she had never been before she walked into that meeting on April 23, 2021.

vi. The Trial

The trial began on July 31, 2024, in Grand Junction. The courtroom was standing room only on the first day. Outside, the city went about its business — peaches ripening on the Western Slope, oil pumps turning in the distance. Inside, prosecutors and defense attorneys delivered opening statements that might as well have been dispatches from two different realities.

For the prosecution, the story was simple. Deputy Attorney General Janet Shapiro told the jury that April 23, 2021 was "the beginning of the conspiracy" — the day Peters met with Douglas Frank and began plotting to bring an unauthorized person into a secure government facility. Everything that followed, Shapiro argued, was deliberate. Peters knew what she was doing. She lied to do it.

For the defense, Peters was a public servant who had acted within her authority, driven by genuine concern about the integrity of the elections she oversaw. Her attorney argued that she had never intended to break the law — that she believed, sincerely and completely, that something was wrong, and that she had a duty to find out what it was.

The jury heard from the people who had been closest to what happened. Belinda Knisley, Peters' former deputy clerk, testified against her as part of her plea agreement. So did Sandra Brown. Both women had worked alongside Peters, had watched the scheme take shape, and had eventually decided that cooperation was the better path. Their testimony placed Peters at the center of the plan — not a bystander, not a pawn, but the person who had made it happen.

Peters did not testify.

The jury was not allowed to hear Peters' evidence about election fraud — the theories, the charts, the data that Conan Hayes had copied from the machines. Judge Matthew Barrett ruled it inadmissible, finding that it had no bearing on whether Peters had committed the crimes she was charged with. Peters would later say the jury had been kept from the truth. The prosecution would say the truth was exactly what the jury heard.

The deliberations lasted two days.

On August 12, 2024, the jury found Peters guilty on seven of ten charges — three felony counts of attempting to influence a public servant, one felony count of conspiracy to commit criminal impersonation, and three misdemeanor counts of official misconduct, violation of duty, and failure to comply with the Secretary of State. She was acquitted on the charges related to identity theft — the jury deciding that Gerald Wood's credentials, used without his full knowledge, did not meet the legal definition of stolen identity.

She was convicted on everything that mattered.

The following morning, Peters appeared on Steve Bannon's War Room podcast to tell her supporters she would keep fighting. She referred to debunked theories about Dominion voting machines. She said the jury hadn't been allowed to hear the evidence.

Sentencing was set for October.

vii. You Are No Hero

On October 3, 2024, Tina Peters stood before Judge Matthew Barrett in the Mesa County District Court and spoke for forty minutes. She had asked for probation. She had prepared remarks. She had photographs.

She showed the judge a picture of her son, Remington — the Navy SEAL who had died in a parachute accident in 2017. She showed him a picture of her ex-husband, Tom, who had died the previous December, his Parkinson's disease having taken him slowly and completely. She talked about her 95-year-old mother in Virginia, who needed her. She talked about her health. She said prison would be a death sentence for a woman her age.

She also, at various points during those forty minutes, tried to introduce the election fraud evidence that had been ruled inadmissible at trial. She talked about Dominion voting machines. She talked about wireless devices and software that she believed had changed ballot images. Judge Barrett interrupted her. "I've let you go on enough about this," he said. "The votes are the votes."

Before Peters spoke, the court heard from others. Matt Crane, executive director of the Colorado County Clerks Association, described death threats made to election officials and their families — threats fueled, he argued, by Peters' conspiracy theories. His own wife and children had been threatened. Thousands of election workers across the country had retired rather than continue doing a job that had become dangerous. "The only fraudulent activity," Crane told the court, "was orchestrated by Tina Peters."

Mesa County Commissioner Cody Davis estimated that Peters' actions had cost the county $1.4 million in legal fees and lost employee time. The county's voting equipment had been decertified and replaced. Its reputation, Davis said, had taken a hit it was still trying to recover from. "She's made a laughingstock of this community," he told the judge.

Douglas Frank, who had paid Peters that first visit on April 23, 2021, spoke in her defense. "I am uniquely qualified to judge Tina Peters' state of mind," he told the court. "It boggles my mind that a Gold Star mother is subjected to this tyranny."

When Peters finished speaking, she told the judge that if he ordered her not to talk about the election, she wouldn't talk about the election. She said she had never done anything with malice. She said she had only wanted to serve the people of Mesa County.

She stood very still when the judge finished speaking.

Barrett did not grant probation. He sentenced her to nine years in the Colorado Department of Corrections. Before he announced the sentence, he spoke directly to Peters for several minutes.

"Your lies are well documented," he told her. "I'm convinced you'd do it all over again if you could. You're as defiant as any defendant this court has ever seen. You are no hero. You're a charlatan who used, and is still using, your prior position in office to peddle a snake oil that's been proven to be junk time and time again."

He told her she had betrayed her oath. He told her prison was the only place for someone like her. He told her she had never taken her job seriously.

She was handcuffed immediately after sentencing and led out of the courtroom. Her supporters, who had filled the gallery, watched her go. One of them — Dee Valdez Pepper, a friend who had met Peters over a smear of lipstick at a Lincoln Day dinner — later described the moment. "She just turned and looked at all of us," Pepper said, "just like, please, is this really happening?"

It was.

viii. The Machine Keeps Running

The handcuffs went on, and the movement got to work.

Within hours of the sentencing, right-wing media had a new martyr. Peters called into Steve Bannon's podcast from jail. Mike Lindell issued a statement. The Selection Code documentary got a fresh wave of attention. On forums and Substacks and Truth Social, the narrative that had been building for three years — that Tina Peters was a sacrificial lamb, chosen by the deep state to frighten election clerks into silence — calcified into something that felt, to its believers, like settled fact.

In December 2025, President Donald Trump announced he had pardoned Tina Peters. Legal experts noted, with some patience, that presidential pardons do not apply to state convictions. Peters had been convicted in a Colorado state court, sentenced by a Colorado state judge, and was serving time in a Colorado state prison. Trump's pardon was, in a technical sense, a piece of paper.

It was not, however, nothing. Trump's public support for Peters gave the movement fresh energy — and gave Trump a lever. He began pressuring Colorado Governor Jared Polis to release her, and when Polis declined, the Trump administration began cutting federal funding to Colorado. The state filed a lawsuit alleging a coordinated campaign of retaliation. Millions of dollars in aid and grants disappeared. The pressure was real, and it was applied without subtlety.

Meanwhile, Peters' attorneys pursued her appeal through the courts. In January 2026, a three-judge panel heard oral arguments. On April 2, 2026, the Colorado Court of Appeals issued a 77-page ruling that managed to satisfy almost no one completely.

The court upheld her conviction on all counts. The evidence was sufficient. The trial had been fair. Tina Peters was guilty.

But the sentencing, the court found, was another matter. Judge Barrett had made a legal error — not in finding her guilty, but in how he had punished her. Several of his statements during sentencing, the appeals judges wrote, "can be read only as the infliction of punishment because of Peters's beliefs and statements about election fraud." Her continued public insistence that the election had been stolen was protected speech. A judge cannot punish someone for their opinions, even wrong ones, even dangerous ones.

The sentence was sent back to the district court for a do-over.

The appeals court also addressed Trump's pardon directly, in language that left little room for interpretation: the presidential pardon "does not reach Peters's state offenses." It was, the court said, meaningless.

Colorado Attorney General Phil Weiser released a statement. "Whatever happens with her sentence," he said, "Tina Peters will always be a convicted felon who violated her duty as Mesa County clerk, put other lives at risk, and threatened our democracy."

The resentencing had not yet been scheduled. The federal pressure had not let up. And in the governor's office in Denver, Jared Polis was reviewing a clemency application.

ix. "Free Tina"

On May 15, 2026, Governor Jared Polis commuted Tina Peters' sentence. He had reviewed her clemency application. He had read, he said, the appeals court's finding that her sentence had been influenced by her protected speech. He had concluded that nine years was an outlier — too long for the crime, shaped too much by the judge's contempt for her beliefs.

He was careful about what he was and wasn't saying. "She committed a crime," Polis told reporters. "She deserves to be a convicted felon. She deserves to do the time. But what happened — and I agree with the appeals court — she has unpopular, and in my opinion, incorrect opinions. Those should not be a factor in her sentencing."

He was not pardoning her. He was not saying she was innocent. He was saying the punishment had been shaped, at least in part, by a judge's anger at what she believed — and that was not how the law was supposed to work.

Peters, her attorneys said, had told Polis in her clemency application that she had made a mistake and would follow the law going forward. It was a striking reversal from the woman who had told a courtroom, eighteen months earlier, that she would do it all again. Whether the admission was sincere, or strategic, or both, is a question only Peters can answer.

The reaction was immediate and predictable and loud. Donald Trump posted "FREE TINA" on Truth Social. The election-denial movement celebrated. Sherronna Bishop went live on Facebook. The prayer call that Dee Valdez Pepper had been running every Thursday for nearly two years — thirty-five people from twelve states, calling in to pray for a woman they had never met — finally had something to celebrate.

On the other side, the condemnations came just as fast. Colorado Secretary of State Jena Griswold said the commutation "will validate and embolden the election denial movement, and leave a dark, dangerous imprint on American democracy for years to come." All 66 Democrats in the Colorado General Assembly signed a letter opposing the decision.

Dan Rubinstein, the Mesa County District Attorney who had prosecuted the case — a Republican — was perhaps the most pointed. "The Governor chose to substitute his judgment for the courts, the sentencing judge, and the Mesa County community that bore the consequences of Ms. Peters' actions," he said. "It is especially troubling that notoriety, political pressure and powerful allies appear to have produced special treatment that ordinary defendants would never receive."

In Grand Junction, Bobbie Gross — who had lost to Peters in the 2018 primary, who had helped hold the clerk's office together after Peters was removed, who had been elected to the job herself in 2023 — was still doing the work. She had opened ballot counting to public observers, trained election judges in front of the community, installed new surveillance systems, held hand counts to prove the machines were accurate. She had answered constituent questions. She had rebuilt, slowly, the trust that Peters had spent eighteen months destroying.

"There's still a lot of distrust," Gross said.

There probably will be for a while.

As of this writing, Tina Peters is eligible for parole on June 1, 2026. She is a convicted felon. She is also, to a significant portion of the country, a hero — a woman who saw something wrong and paid for it with years of her life.

What is not in dispute is what happened in Mesa County. The ballots left in the parking lot. The meeting on April 23. The man who walked into a secure facility under a borrowed name and walked out with hard drives full of sensitive data. The staff who were interviewed by the FBI and never quite recovered. The $1.4 million. The election workers who quit rather than keep doing a job that had become threatening. The community that is still, years later, trying to convince its own residents that their votes are counted honestly.

Tina Peters did not set out to do any of that. She set out to prove something. She never did.
