Who Is Tina Peters to Those Who Know Her?

The allies, the patrons, the believers — and the conspiracy they built around her. Operation Sacrificial Lamb, Mike Lindell, and the machinery of election denial.

~25 min read 6 sections Includes composite interviews
i. The Patron

In the months after the Mesa County election security breach became public, Tina Peters did not lack for resources. Her legal defense was funded. Her story was filmed. Her travel was covered. Behind most of it was one person: MyPillow CEO Mike Lindell.

Campaign finance complaints filed against Peters and later substantiated by the Colorado Secretary of State's office showed she had received significant financial support from Lindell and affiliated organizations. As an elected official, Peters was prohibited under Colorado law from receiving gifts. The violations contributed to a $15,400 fine levied against her in October 2023.

Lindell also paid for Douglas Frank's travel to Grand Junction for the April 23, 2021 meeting that prosecutors called the beginning of the conspiracy. A secret recording of that meeting, played at Peters' trial, captured Frank telling those present that Lindell funded his nationwide tour of county election offices.

In 2022, Lindell produced a documentary about Peters called Selection Code. The film cast Peters as a whistleblower who had accidentally discovered evidence of a global election manipulation scheme. It did not mention Conan Hayes, the unauthorized person Peters had smuggled into the trusted build. It did not mention that the copied data had been leaked online. Reviewers noted the film's claims had not held up against subsequent reporting and legal proceedings.

Lindell also flew Peters to his "cyber symposium" in Sioux Falls, South Dakota in August 2021 — the event she attended while her office was being raided by federal investigators.

Daniel Mercer, a campaign finance researcher who has tracked election-denial funding networks, said Lindell's investment in Peters followed a pattern seen across multiple states.

"Lindell identified local officials who were sympathetic to his claims and provided them with resources — legal support, media platforms, travel," Mercer said. "Peters was one of the most prominent examples, but she wasn't the only one. The model was to find someone with institutional access and amplify them."

What Lindell received in return was content. Peters' story — a local clerk stumbling onto evidence of a global conspiracy — was exactly the kind of narrative his movement needed. Her institutional access, her willingness to go public and her subsequent prosecution gave the election-denial movement a martyr with a paper trail.

Sheila Rae, the former Mesa County Clerk who succeeded Peters temporarily after her removal, said the financial relationship was an open secret in Grand Junction.

"She gets paid for those podcasts," Rae said. "It's on the books that she received money from Lindell and that entire group. She also accepted private jet rides from him. Who knows how much she actually received from donations — she's constantly raising money."

ii. The Brokers

Tina Peters did not act alone. The network surrounding her included a former congressional campaign manager, a traveling mathematician, a retired surfer with connections to a separate election breach in Georgia and a Florida attorney who drafted a model executive order for the Trump White House.

Sherronna Bishop, who brokered the April 23, 2021 meeting between Peters and Douglas Frank, had managed Lauren Boebert's successful 2020 congressional campaign in Colorado's 3rd District. She ran a Facebook livestream called "The Patriot Call" and had established relationships with national election-denial figures before approaching Peters.

Douglas Frank, the Ohio mathematician Lindell paid to travel the country, provided Peters with what supporters described as mathematical proof of election fraud. His methodology was rejected by professional statisticians and election officials who reviewed his work. He continued presenting it regardless and appeared at Peters' sentencing in October 2024 to speak on her behalf.

"I am uniquely qualified to judge Tina Peters' state of mind," Frank told the court. "It boggles my mind that a Gold Star mother is subjected to this tyranny."

Conan James Hayes, the retired California surfer Peters smuggled into the trusted build using Gerald Wood's credentials, was later linked by investigators to a similar breach of election equipment in Coffee County, Georgia. The parallel operation used the same general approach — unauthorized access to Dominion voting machines during a maintenance procedure — and involved some of the same people. The connection suggested the Mesa County breach was not improvised but part of a coordinated national effort.

Peter Ticktin, Peters' defense attorney, drafted a model executive order for President Trump in coordination with Michael Flynn and Patrick Byrne that would have directed federal resources toward investigating election fraud claims. The order was never signed. The drafting placed Peters' legal team at the highest levels of the post-2020 election-denial movement.

Sarah Voss, a former Mesa County Republican campaign worker, said the network around Peters was more organized than it appeared from the outside.

"These weren't random people who found each other," Voss said. "Bishop knew Frank. Frank knew Lindell. Lindell knew Ticktin. Peters was the local access point they needed. She may have believed she was running her own operation, but she was also being run."

Voss said the structure mirrored other far-right organizing efforts from the same period.

"You had a national funding source, a traveling field operation and local officials who provided institutional legitimacy," Voss said. "It's a model. Mesa County was one node in a much larger network."

iii. Operation Sacrificial Lamb

Among Peters' supporters, her prosecution has a name: Operation Sacrificial Lamb.

The term does not appear in any official court document or law enforcement record. It originated in the right-wing media ecosystem that grew up around Peters' case and was adopted by her supporters as a shorthand for a specific theory: that Peters was deliberately targeted by state and federal officials to make an example of her and discourage other election officials from questioning voting systems.

Dee Valdez Pepper, a Republican activist from Elbert County who became one of Peters' closest supporters, laid out the theory in an interview.

"This was an operation that the county commissioners put in place in Mesa County to frame Tina Peters," Valdez Pepper said. "So that they could put the fear of God in all the clerks around the country and prevent anyone from asking any questions."

Valdez Pepper said evidence for the operation came partly from a Mesa County commissioner meeting she claimed was secretly recorded. She said her husband used audio software to isolate a whispered exchange between commissioners that she believes contained the phrase "sacrificial lamb" — used as a trigger word to initiate a search warrant against Peters.

Mesa County Commissioner Cody Davis, who spoke at Peters' sentencing and called her actions an embarrassment to the community, has not publicly responded to the sacrificial lamb allegation. The Grand Junction Daily Sentinel found no evidence supporting the account.

Valdez Pepper said she believes the prosecution extended to the highest levels of state government. She named Colorado Secretary of State Jena Griswold, Attorney General Phil Weiser and Judge Matthew Barrett as participants in the operation.

"They chose Tina to be the sacrificial lamb," Valdez Pepper said. "They needed somebody to be the example."

The theory has circulated widely in right-wing media. Joe Hoft's Gateway Pundit published multiple pieces framing Peters' incarceration as political persecution. A video titled "Sacrificial Lamb: The Tina Peters Story" was posted to Truth Social in January 2026. Trump's "FREE TINA" posts fit neatly into the same narrative.

Griswold, who launched the investigation into the breach after the copied data appeared online, rejected the characterization.

"The only person who created this situation is Tina Peters," Griswold said following Peters' conviction. "She made choices that compromised the security of Mesa County's election system and harmed voters and election workers across the state."

Rae, who helped run the office after Peters was removed, said the theory inverts the actual sequence of events.

"The Mesa County elections were tabulated correctly and had been audited in a number of ways," Rae said. "The State of Colorado has some of the most stringent auditing requirements in the nation, and the 2020 elections passed every single audit and more. The big lie is to say that any of our elections were fraudulent or manipulated in any way."

Tina Peters discovered evidence of a global election manipulation scheme through the 2021 Mesa County trusted build. What the record shows

Peters signed documents in November 2020 certifying Mesa County's election results contained no discrepancies. She changed her position after meeting with Douglas Frank — a paid operative of Mike Lindell — in April 2021. Colorado's 2020 elections passed every state-required audit. No court, investigator or independent expert has validated Peters' election fraud claims.

What supporters claim

During a county commissioner meeting, a commissioner whispered a secret code word — "sacrificial lamb" — while his microphone was still live. The phrase was a trigger: it signaled law enforcement to move forward with a search warrant to seize Peters' iPad and manufacture evidence against her.

What the record shows

The Grand Junction Daily Sentinel found no evidence supporting this account. Commissioner Cody Davis, who spoke at Peters' sentencing and called her actions an embarrassment to the community, has not publicly responded to the allegation. The search warrant for Peters' iPad was related to a documented incident in which she was observed recording a court proceeding.

What supporters claim

Authorities staged Peters' arrest in public to generate photographs. Those images were used repeatedly in local media to poison the jury pool before her trial. She was convicted not on evidence but on pre-existing bias created by more than 700 negative front-page news stories published before the trial began.

What the record shows

Peters' defense attorneys filed motions related to pretrial publicity. The court oversaw a jury selection process. Peters was convicted by a jury of Mesa County peers on seven of ten charges after a trial in which both sides presented evidence. Her own deputies testified against her under oath.

What supporters claim

Peters was chosen specifically to be made an example — a sacrificial lamb — by state officials working with Dominion Voting Systems to frighten election clerks across the country into silence. Her nine-year sentence was designed not as punishment but as a warning to anyone who might question voting machines.

What the record shows

The Colorado Court of Appeals upheld Peters' conviction unanimously in April 2026 but partially overturned her sentence — finding that Judge Barrett had improperly factored her protected speech into his sentencing. DA Dan Rubinstein, a Republican, said Peters received special treatment due to her notoriety, not the reverse.

What supporters claim

Peters' release proves the system is beginning to correct itself. Her commutation is vindication. Trump's pressure on Colorado demonstrates that the truth she uncovered is finally being taken seriously at the highest levels of government.

What the record shows

Governor Polis granted clemency while explicitly affirming Peters' guilt and her felon status. Her conviction was not overturned. Secretary of State Griswold said the commutation would embolden election deniers. DA Rubinstein said notoriety and political pressure produced special treatment no ordinary defendant would receive.

iv. The Woman Who Took Her Job

When Tina Peters was suspended from her election duties in September 2021, two people were appointed to replace her on the same night by two different authorities — the Secretary of State and the county commissioners each named their own choice, setting off a jurisdictional dispute over who had the power to fill the vacancy.

Sheila Rae was the Secretary of State's appointee. Wayne Williams was the county commissioners'. Rather than let the state and county fight it out, Rae and Williams made a decision.

"He and I just made a pact and said it doesn't really matter who appointed who — we'll just work together," Rae said. "That's how I came into the role of election supervisor in her absence."

The office Rae walked into was not the one she had left three years earlier.

"There was a lot of trauma on the staff level," Rae said. "There are good-hearted people at the county trying to just do their job, and this mass chaos is happening. The FBI is in the office. The voting equipment that they relied on to work — to build ballots, send out ballots, receive ballots, count ballots — is all quarantined. The county is without voting equipment. Just tons of angst."

Staff were being interviewed by federal investigators. Equipment sat under quarantine. Military ballots needed to go out within ten days. Rae said she and Williams focused on keeping the office functional and the remaining staff calm.

"It was really, really important for Wayne and I to always just stay super calm and reassure the staff that they're on the right track and that we're doing the right things," she said.

The damage to staff was lasting. Out of approximately 30 people who had worked in the office when Rae left in 2018, three remained by the time Peters was removed.

"That's the kind of turnover she caused," Rae said. "I feel badly for our county, for the reputation, for the employees that were either forced out or chose to leave their job because it was no longer a good fit for them — who, previous to that, enjoyed their work and did a good job for the citizens of Mesa County."

With public trust in Mesa County's elections severely damaged, the county commissioners authorized Rae to conduct the next election — a 2021 school district vote — in three ways simultaneously to demonstrate accuracy. The ballots were tabulated on Dominion equipment as required by law, then rescanned by Clear Ballot, a competing vendor, and then hand-counted by more than 100 election judges over the course of a week.

"All three counts resulted in the exact same result," Rae said. "Nothing that changed the results of the election and nothing that was nefarious."

Linda Torres, one of the election judges who participated in the hand count, said the process was exhausting but necessary.

"We sat there for days going through those ballots," Torres said. "Every single one. And the numbers matched. I don't know what else you could do to prove it."

The response from Peters' supporters was dismissive. Rae said they argued the results matched because officials knew they were being watched.

"Those that are in the Tina camp — their response to us validating that election was, 'Well, of course it matched, because now they know we're watching them,'" Rae said. "So it did no good to convince the naysayers."

Rae said she has largely given up trying.

"I've tried for a long time," she said. "I think they're going to believe what they believe. And so we just have to keep doing the job that we're doing and not let that noise deter us."

The noise has not stopped. Rae and current Clerk Bobbi Gross are still named regularly on Peters' podcast and on Sherronna Bishop's Facebook livestream, where supporters allege they worked for Dominion voting machines and conspired to frame Peters.

"Their theory is that Bobbi and I had this conspiracy where we worked for Dominion voting equipment and we set her up," Rae said. "It's noise. It's annoying. It's lies being said about you."

One federal resource that had helped county election officials monitor online threats is no longer available. The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency — CISA — which provided counties with information about potential cyber threats and social media chatter related to elections, was defunded by the Trump administration in 2025.

"It was a layer of awareness that was really nice to have, and that's gone away now," Rae said. "It's just more and more imperative that the local election officials and the counties hold the line to keep the elections running as smoothly as possible for the citizens."

v. Her Close Friend

Dee Valdez Pepper met Tina Peters at a Lincoln Day dinner in Elbert County in 2022. Peters was campaigning for Secretary of State. She was standing in the back of the room when Valdez Pepper noticed she had lipstick on her teeth.

"I just said, oh, you got a little bit of lipstick on your teeth right here," Valdez Pepper recalled. "And she rubbed and she just said, 'Oh, that's so sweet. That's what a girlfriend would do.' And I said, 'Well, you have a new girlfriend.'"

That was a Saturday. The following Monday was Memorial Day — the anniversary of the death of Peters' son Remington, a Navy SEAL who died in a parachute accident in 2017. Peters began texting Valdez Pepper. She sent pictures. She sent videos.

"She was a grieving mom," Valdez Pepper said. "And I just was her friend."

Over the following months, Valdez Pepper said she learned the details of Peters' case and became a supporter. Peters asked her to help organize support for her friends in Mesa County, who Valdez Pepper said were exhausted and frightened.

"She said, 'Will you find a way to support my friends in Mesa County? They're so weary. They've been through so much. They're so afraid all the time because there's so much corruption there,'" Valdez Pepper recalled.

Valdez Pepper started a weekly prayer call. It runs every Thursday at 12:10 p.m. by conference call. She said it now draws roughly 35 people from more than 12 states.

"Her friends are a lot stronger now," Valdez Pepper said. "They're not as afraid. They know that people have their backs."

Valdez Pepper attended Peters' sentencing hearing in October 2024. She sat in the front row of the gallery when Judge Barrett handed down the nine-year sentence and officers moved to take Peters into custody.

"She just turned and looked at all of us," Valdez Pepper said. "Just like, please, is this really happening?"

After Peters was transferred to the Denver correctional facility, Valdez Pepper said Peters remained active. She called into a Colorado GOP fundraising dinner hosted by Steve Bannon from inside the facility.

"I don't know how she's so strong," Valdez Pepper said. "She's a very strong woman of God. She has more faith than anyone I've ever seen in my life. She's led her cellmates to Christ. She's a beacon of light where she is."

Valdez Pepper said she believes Peters' admission to Governor Polis that she made a mistake — the statement that secured her commutation — did not reflect a genuine change of position.

"She said what she needed to say to get out," Valdez Pepper said. "That doesn't mean she's changed her mind about any of it."

Valdez Pepper said she intends to keep running the Thursday prayer call after Peters' release.

"What can happen to Tina can happen to any of us," she said. "And that's why her case is so important. It has to be made right."

vi. The Family Dissenters

Not everyone in Peters' family shared her supporters' view of her prosecution. Jack Peters, the brother of her ex-husband Thomas, described himself to the Grand Junction Daily Sentinel as "extremely conservative" — and called Tina Peters "vile."

"I've always been a bit at odds with Tina," Jack Peters told the Sentinel. "She's always been somewhat abrasive."

Jack Peters disputed Peters' public account of her role in her ex-husband's construction business, which she has described as a significant management position. He said her involvement amounted to part-time bookkeeping at best.

He also accused her of exploiting Remington Peters' death for financial gain. Peters regularly invokes her son's memory at public appearances and in fundraising appeals, describing herself as a Gold Star mother — a designation that formally applies to families of service members killed in combat. Remington Peters died in a parachute accident at a public air show.

"She is unfairly using her former husband and her deceased son to gain sympathy in her criminal case," Jack Peters told the Sentinel.

Thomas Peters filed a civil lawsuit against Tina alleging she had used a fraudulent power of attorney to transfer ownership of a home he had purchased after their separation into her name. She filed the deed transfer in the recording office she controlled as clerk. Thomas Peters died Dec. 31, 2023, before the civil case was resolved.

Sheila Rae, who knew Peters through the clerk's office, said accounts of Peters' behavior extended beyond her public role. Rae said a neighbor told her that a buyer who had purchased a home Peters had flipped arrived to find the brand-new appliances had been replaced with used ones after the final walkthrough.

"She really did live in an unethical way," Rae said. "And it wasn't just about our office."

Peters has not publicly responded to the appliance allegation. Jack Peters said the family's experience of her was consistent with the public record.

"My brother-in-law and my sister are being unjustly persecuted by this vile woman," he told the Sentinel.

v. Bookmarking the saga

Bobbi Gross has been Mesa County Clerk and Recorder since January 2023. She lost to Peters in the 2018 Republican primary — the race that started everything — by roughly 400 votes. She spent the next four years watching from the outside as Peters dismantled the office Gross had spent a decade preparing to run.

Since taking over, Gross has opened ballot counting to public observers, trained election judges in front of the community, installed new surveillance systems and conducted hand counts to demonstrate machine accuracy. She has answered constituent questions at public meetings, published transparency reports and made herself available in ways her predecessor did not.

"We have been doing a lot of work in Mesa County to try to show the public how safe and secure this process is," Gross said. "But there's still a lot of distrust."

Patricia Holden, a Mesa County Republican who has voted in every election since 1988, said she requested to observe the ballot counting process for the first time in 2024.

"I never felt like I needed to before," Holden said. "Now I do. Not because I think Bobbi is doing anything wrong — I think she's doing everything right. But after what happened, I just need to see it for myself."

Rae, who helped hold the office together after Peters was removed, said the damage Peters caused will take years to fully repair.

"This is going to be in our history books," Rae said. "Two or three presidents down the road, maybe this will just be history. I don't know."

The legal case that produced that history closed — provisionally — on May 15, 2026, when Governor Polis commuted Peters' sentence. Peters is eligible for parole June 1, 2026.

The commutation drew sharp condemnation from officials on both sides of the aisle. Dan Rubinstein, the Mesa County District Attorney who prosecuted the case and who is himself a Republican, said the outcome reflected the influence of Peters' national profile rather than the merits of her case.

"Notoriety, political pressure and powerful allies appear to have produced special treatment that ordinary defendants would never receive," Rubinstein said.

Colorado Secretary of State Jena Griswold said the commutation would have consequences beyond Peters herself.

"This will validate and embolden the election denial movement," Griswold said, "and leave a dark, dangerous imprint on American democracy for years to come."

Polis defended the decision. "She committed a crime," he said. "She deserves to be a convicted felon. She deserves to do the time. But she has unpopular, and in my opinion, incorrect opinions. Those should not be a factor in her sentencing."

Peters' criminal conviction was not overturned. She remains a convicted felon. The voting equipment she compromised has been replaced. The staff she drove out have mostly moved on. The $1.4 million Mesa County spent responding to her actions is gone.

Valdez Pepper said she will keep running the Thursday prayer call after Peters is released. She said the group is already planning a welcome-home gathering.

"What can happen to Tina can happen to any of us," she said. "That's why we're not going anywhere."

In Grand Junction, Bobbi Gross continues to show up. She answers questions. She opens the counting room. She publishes the reports. She does, in other words, the job.

"There's still a lot of distrust out there," Gross said. "We just have to keep going."
